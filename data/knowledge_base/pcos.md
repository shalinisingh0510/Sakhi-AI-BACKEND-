# Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) Clinical Knowledge

## Overview of PCOS
Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) is a common endocrine and metabolic disorder affecting between 6% and 12% of women of reproductive age worldwide. It is characterized by hormonal imbalances, irregular ovulatory function, and metabolic risk factors.

## Diagnostic Criteria (Rotterdam Consensus)
A clinical diagnosis of PCOS requires the presence of at least two of the following three features, after excluding other conditions (such as thyroid dysfunction, hyperprolactinemia, and congenital adrenal hyperplasia):
1. **Oligo-ovulation or Anovulation**: Infrequent, irregular, or completely absent menstrual periods.
2. **Clinical or Biochemical Hyperandrogenism**: Signs of elevated androgen levels, such as excess facial/body hair (hirsutism), severe acne, male-pattern hair thinning, or elevated free testosterone on blood tests.
3. **Polycystic Ovaries on Ultrasound**: The presence of 12 or more follicles (measuring 2–9 mm in diameter) in one or both ovaries, or increased ovarian volume (>10 mL).

*Note: Self-diagnosis is not possible based on symptoms alone. A formal medical workup involving blood tests and pelvic ultrasound by a qualified physician is required.*

## Management and Lifestyle Guidance
- **Dietary Approaches**: A balanced, low-glycemic index diet rich in whole grains, fiber, and healthy fats improves insulin sensitivity.
- **Regular Physical Activity**: Moderate physical activity for 150 minutes per week helps regulate insulin, improves ovulatory regularity, and supports mental wellbeing.
- **Medical Therapies**: Physicians may prescribe combined oral contraceptives to regulate cycles and protect the endometrium, or metformin to manage insulin resistance.
